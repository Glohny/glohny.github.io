How it's built

        

          
I modeled the whole machine in Fusion 360 and 3D printed every structural part. I only bought what has to be precise or carry the motion: linear rods, bearings, pulleys, carriages, motors and electronics.

          
The X axis has two belts, one on each side of the gantry, both driven by one motor through a shared shaft. The pen lift is a servo with a spring-loaded holder: the servo lifts and lowers the pen, and the spring supplies the down-force, so the pen follows uneven paper without the servo having to be precise.

          
Drawings go from Inkscape through a G-code extension into Universal G-code Sender, which streams them to MIGRBL on an Arduino Uno. My servo is mounted the opposite way from the reference build, so M3 S40 lifts the pen and M5 lowers it.

The 25% error

        
I told the plotter to draw a 100 mm square and the bottom edge came out 125.8 mm. Steps/mm was set to 50, a guessed value. Drag the slider to see what that setting does.

        

          
          
          50.0
        

        

          
          
        

        

        
Instead of just dividing by the error, I worked the value out from the hardware:

        

          steps/mm =
          200 steps/rev × 8 microsteps20 teeth × 2 mm pitch
          = 40
        

        
50 ÷ 40 = 1.25 is a clean hardware ratio, not measurement noise, which is why the error was almost exactly 25%. Any constant that turns firmware commands into physical motion should be worked out from the hardware first, then checked by measuring.

      

      

        
      

    

  






  

    
Other problems I solved

    

      

        
Gantry racking

        
Circles weren't closing. With two X belts on one motor, unequal tension lets one end of the gantry respond faster, so it sits off square. Both belts run on the same layout, so I cut them to exactly the same length: equal stretch, equal tension. The diagonal test later showed the frame square to within 0.5°.

      

      

        
Spikes from arc commands

        
Complicated drawings had random spikes and misplaced details. Jogging by hand was accurate, which pointed at the G-code. The extension turned curves into G2/G3 arcs with huge center offsets. Flattening curves into short G1 moves fixed it.

      

      

        
Pen drag

        
The printed pen carriage needs a loose fit on its rod to slide, but that play let the rod sway, so the pen dragged instead of lifting cleanly. Tape on the rod tightened the fit without binding. It worked well, so I kept it.

      

    

  






  

    

      
How I measured it

      
Each test pattern checks one failure mode. I measured with a ruler first, then checked with calipers.

      
Squareness from the diagonals. If the axes are skewed by a small angle θ, the square becomes a parallelogram and its diagonals differ by about s√2·θ. For a 100 mm square, a 0.5° skew would put them about 1.2 mm apart. Both measured 141.42 mm, so under 0.5° is a conservative bound.

      
What limits the numbers. Every result sits near the 0.5 mm width of the pen tip, so the machine is at least as good as my measurements can show. I switched repeatability from crosses to single dots after play in the pen mount caused sticking on tiny moves, and widened the backlash lines from 1 mm to 3 mm apart so the pen width wouldn't blur them together.

      
The test-pattern G-code was generated with AI assistance. I chose the tests, ran them and did all the measuring.

    

    

      
What went wrong

      
24 V into the wrong barrel jack

      
I plugged the 24 V supply into the Uno's barrel jack instead of the CNC shield's. They look identical. It burned out the regulator and killed the USB-to-serial chip. I replaced the Uno and confirmed the shield and drivers had survived. Two identical connectors at different voltages will eventually get swapped, so the real fix is making the wrong connection hard to make.

      
Warped frame

      
The printed frame has since warped enough to take the machine out of service. Getting it working well again means a redesign, not a repair.
